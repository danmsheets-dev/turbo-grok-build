Three launch blockers

Each of these is a claim the repo cannot currently support. In a campaign whose entire premise is honesty, one of them landing publicly does damage the other 29 days can't repair.

Verdict — not ready Full detail in 08-CLAIMS-LEDGER.md §0
01

Every rc.10 link in your README is a 404

VERSION reads 1.0.0-rc.10 and the README documents rc.10 downloads and a pin command — but v1.0.0-rc.9 is still Latest on GitHub. A prospect clicking the download link in your first launch post hits a dead page. Publish the release, or reference rc.9.

02

"Meeting audio never leaves the machine" is false

The in-page tap streams over 127.0.0.1, but that socket feeds a loop streaming to wss://api.x.ai/v1/stt. Your own design spec rejects competitors for exactly this. Corrected in the copy — just never say it in an unscripted moment.

03

"Every agent runs in an isolated worktree" is false

The explore, plan, and oracle types default to the shared workspace, and named tests assert it. Corrected to "write-capable agents" — a better sentence anyway, because it shows the permission model is deliberate rather than blanket.

The strategy

Don't market the app. Market the evidence it provides.

Your repo is a Rust terminal CLI for developers. Your buyer is a VP of Marketing who lives in HubSpot and has never opened a terminal. Those audiences barely overlap — post the app to RevOps and you get silence; post it to developers and you get stars with no pipeline.

But your About page already names the way out. You keep no public profiles, so you prove capability with artifacts: live demos, interactive tools, fixed-scope starters. Public source code is the fifth pillar, and the strongest — because unlike the others, anyone can verify it at 2am without booking a call.

Most "AI automation agencies" are a Zapier account, an OpenAI key, and a Canva deck. You can point at a public multi-agent runtime — including a changelog that documents your own failures in detail.

That gap can't be closed with a better landing page, which is exactly what makes it worth 30 days of content.

What the copy actually reads like

All 52 posts are written to this standard and paste-ready. Every LinkedIn post ships with its first comment; every tweet is verified under 280 characters.

LinkedIn · Day 15 · Monday1,537 characters

A B2B buyer asks about 15 questions before they choose a vendor. Most B2B sites answer 4 of them on a page of their own.

The other 11 get answered anyway. Just not by you.

Ten of the fifteen, roughly in the order they get asked:

  • What does this cost, and what shape is the price?
  • How long until it is actually live?
  • What happens to our data, and where does it sit?
  • Do we have to replace our CRM?
  • Our CRM data is messy. Does that break this?
  • How is this different from the tool we already pay for?
  • Who is this not for?
  • What does my team have to do for this to work?
  • What happens when it breaks and nobody is watching?
  • What does it cost us to do nothing for two more quarters?

Count how many of those have a page on your site. Not a paragraph inside a case study. A page, with a heading that matches the question.

Whatever you did not write, something else did. Usually a competitor comparison page, or a listicle from a site that has never used a single product on it. That is the page an assistant reads from when your buyer asks.

The uncomfortable one is “who is this not for”. Almost nobody builds it. It earns the most trust and it kills the most bad-fit discovery calls.
